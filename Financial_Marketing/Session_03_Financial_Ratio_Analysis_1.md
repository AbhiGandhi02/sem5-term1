# Session 3: Financial Ratio Analysis (Part 1)

> 🎓 In the earlier sessions we learned how financial statements are built. Now we learn to **read** them. Ratios work like a doctor's blood test: a few numbers tell you whether the company is "healthy".

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**
- Explain the purpose and importance of ratio analysis
- Calculate liquidity, efficiency (working capital) and solvency/leverage ratios
- Interpret ratios to judge a company's health, efficiency and risk
- Use ratios to support business decisions

## 1. What are Financial Ratios?

**Financial ratios** are metrics used to evaluate a company's **financial health** and **performance**.
- They are calculated from the **financial statements** (Balance Sheet, P&L).
- They make **comparison** possible: the company's own **past vs present**, and against **industry peers**.

> 💡 **In simple words:** ₹10 Cr profit bada hai ya chhota? Pata nahi, jab tak company ka size na pata ho. Ratio ek number ko doosre se compare karke usse **meaningful** bana deta hai.

### Objectives of Financial Ratios

| Objective | What it checks |
|---|---|
| Assess **profitability** | Is the business earning **enough profit** for owners/stakeholders? |
| Evaluate **liquidity** | Can it pay its **short-term** obligations? |
| Measure **operational efficiency** | Is it using its assets efficiently? |
| Assess **solvency & financial risk** | Can it meet its **long-term** obligations? |
| Check **return on capital employed** | What return do **owners and lenders** earn on their money? |
| **Performance comparison** | Against **time, competitors, industry, budget** |
| Support **managerial decision-making** | Data-based decisions |

---

## 2. The 5 Types of Ratios

| Category | Main question |
|---|---|
| **Liquidity Ratios** | Can we pay short-term obligations? |
| **Efficiency Ratios** | How efficiently are assets and working capital used? |
| **Solvency / Leverage Ratios** | Can we pay long-term obligations? How much financial risk is there? |
| **Profitability & Return Ratios** | How effectively are profits and returns generated? |
| **Market / Investor Ratios** | How is the business performing in the eyes of shareholders and the stock market? |

> 💡 Memory trick **"LESPM"**: **L**iquidity (today's bills), **E**fficiency (speed of work), **S**olvency (the long race), **P**rofitability (earnings), **M**arket (investor mood).

## 3. Liquidity Ratios 💧

**Liquidity** = whether the company can pay its **short-term obligations** (due within 1 year) **when they fall due**.

> *"Does the business have enough short-term assets and cash-generating capacity to pay its short-term liabilities?"*

| Short-term Liabilities (due within 1 year) | Short-term Assets (used to pay them) |
|---|---|
| Trade payables / creditors | Cash & cash equivalents |
| Short-term borrowings | Bank balances |
| Bank overdraft | Short-term investments / marketable securities |
| Accrued expenses | Trade receivables |
| Outstanding wages & salaries | Inventory |
| Taxes payable | Other current assets (e.g. prepaid expenses) |
| **Current portion of long-term debt** | |

> 💡 **In simple words:** 5 saal ke loan ki jo EMI **agle 12 mahine** mein deni hai, wo current liability hai. Baaki non-current hai.

### 3A. Current Ratio

$$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

- **Current Assets** = Cash & bank, inventories, trade receivables, prepaid expenses, short-term investments
- **Current Liabilities** = Trade payables, bank overdraft, short-term borrowings, accrued expenses, other short-term obligations

**Meaning:** How many ₹ of current assets the company has for every ₹1 of current liabilities.

| What high means | What low means |
|---|---|
| Short-term bills can be paid comfortably ✅ | May struggle to pay bills ⚠️ |
| ⚠️ If **too** high: idle cash or piles of stock (money isn't working) | Below 1: current liabilities exceed current assets |

> 💡 The traditional rule of thumb is **2:1**, but it varies by industry. Supermarkets often have a CR below 1 and are still healthy.

### 3B. Quick Ratio (Acid-Test Ratio)

$$\text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quick Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}, \quad \text{Quick Assets} = \text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory}$$

**Why?** Inventory takes time to sell and **can't always be turned into cash easily**. This ratio checks whether bills can be paid **without selling inventory**.

| What high means | What low means |
|---|---|
| Liabilities are covered even without selling inventory ✅ | Inventory must be sold to pay the bills ⚠️ |

> 💡 Rule of thumb **1:1**. If the Current Ratio is high but the Quick Ratio is low, most of the money is **stuck in inventory**.
> Some textbooks also remove prepaid expenses from quick assets. The slide's formula removes only inventory; follow the slide in exams.

### 3C. Cash Ratio (the strictest)

$$\text{Cash Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash + Cash Equivalents + Marketable Securities}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

Only **cash and highly liquid securities** count. Receivables and inventory don't.

| What high means | What low means |
|---|---|
| All bills could be paid in cash today ✅ | Depends on customer payments or inventory sales |
| ⚠️ If too high, cash is sitting idle | Most companies are below 1; this is normal |

> 🧠 **Strictness ladder:** Current Ratio (all current assets) → Quick Ratio (remove inventory) → Cash Ratio (only cash + securities). The ratio gets **smaller** as you go down.

## 4. Efficiency Ratios ⚙️ (Activity / Asset-Management Ratios)

These show how effectively the business uses its **assets and working capital** to generate **sales/revenue**.

> *"How efficiently is the company turning its resources into revenue?"*

The business invests money in **inventory, trade receivables, working capital, fixed assets and total assets**. Efficiency ratios check whether these resources are used **productively**.

> 💡 **In simple words:** **Turnover ratio** = "saal mein kitni baar ghooma?" (times). **Days ratio** = "ek chakkar mein kitne din lage?" (days).
> Shortcut: **Days = 365 ÷ Turnover**

### 4A. Inventory Turnover Ratio & Inventory Holding Period

$$\text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average Inventory}}, \quad \text{Avg Inventory} = \frac{\text{Opening + Closing Inventory}}{2}$$

$$\text{Inventory Holding Period (Days Inventory Outstanding)} = \frac{\text{Average Inventory}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365$$

**Meaning:** How many times inventory is **sold and replaced** in a year, and how many days stock sits in the warehouse on average.

| High turnover / Fewer days | Low turnover / More days |
|---|---|
| Goods sell quickly, stock stays fresh ✅ | Goods sit idle; risk of slow-moving/obsolete stock ⚠️ |
| ⚠️ If too high: risk of stock-outs (customer comes, no goods) | Money is stuck in inventory, plus storage cost |

> 💡 The numerator is **COGS**, not Sales, because inventory is recorded **at cost**. Using Sales would sneak the profit margin in and give a wrong (higher) ratio.

### 4B. Receivables Turnover Ratio & Average Collection Period

$$\text{Receivables Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Credit Sales}}{\text{Average Trade Receivables}}, \quad \text{Avg Receivables} = \frac{\text{Opening + Closing}}{2}$$

$$\text{Average Collection Period (DSO / Debtor Days)} = \frac{\text{Average Trade Receivables}}{\text{Net Credit Sales}} \times 365$$

**Meaning:** How many times receivables are **converted into cash** in a year, and how many days customers take to pay on average.

| High turnover / Low DSO | Low turnover / High DSO |
|---|---|
| Customers pay quickly; strong collection ✅ | Payments come late; risk of bad debts ⚠️ |
| ⚠️ A very strict credit policy may hurt sales | DSO above credit terms (e.g. 30 days) means weak collection |

> 💡 Use **Net Credit Sales**, because cash sales never create a receivable.

### 4C. Payables Turnover Ratio & Average Payment Period

$$\text{Payables Turnover} = \frac{\text{Credit Purchases}}{\text{Average Trade Payables}}, \quad \text{Avg Payables} = \frac{\text{Opening + Closing}}{2}$$

$$\text{Average Payment Period (DPO / Payables Days)} = \frac{\text{Average Trade Payables}}{\text{Credit Purchases}} \times 365$$

> 📌 **If credit purchases are not available, COGS can be used.**

**Meaning:** How quickly the company **pays its suppliers**.

| High turnover / Low DPO | Low turnover / High DPO |
|---|---|
| Pays suppliers quickly; good reputation ✅ | Uses suppliers' money to run the business (free financing) ✅ |
| But its own cash leaves quickly | ⚠️ If too high, it may signal a cash shortage; suppliers may get upset |

> ⚠️ The slide's definition of Average Payment Period is cut off ("the average number of..."). Full line: *the average number of days the company takes to pay its suppliers.*

### 4D. Working Capital Cycle (Operating Cycle) 🔄

This is the **time** it takes for money invested in **day-to-day operations to come back as cash**.

$$\text{Cycle (days)} = \text{Inventory Holding Period} + \text{Receivable Days} - \text{Payable Days}$$

```
Goods from supplier (credit) → Sits in warehouse (DIO) → Sold, customer owes (DSO) → Cash received
            └──── Payment to supplier (DPO) ────┘
```

| Short cycle | Long cycle |
|---|---|
| Money comes back fast; less working capital needed ✅ | More working capital needed; short-term loans required ⚠️ |
| **Negative** cycle: cash from customers arrives before suppliers are paid (supermarkets, e-commerce) | |

**How to shorten the cycle?** Sell inventory faster (DIO ⬇️), collect from customers faster (DSO ⬇️), get longer credit from suppliers (DPO ⬆️).

> ⚠️ **Naming note:** Textbooks call `DIO + DSO` the **Operating Cycle**, and `DIO + DSO − DPO` the **Cash Conversion Cycle (net operating cycle)**. The slide uses both names for the same formula. Write the slide's formula in exams, but remember the difference.

## 5. Solvency / Leverage Ratios 🏗️

These measure the company's ability to meet its **long-term obligations**.

> *"Is the company's debt level sustainable? Can it pay long-term interest and principal?"*

| Liquidity | Solvency |
|---|---|
| **Short term** (1 year) | **Long term** |
| "Can we pay next month's bill?" | "Will this company survive 10 years? Can it repay the loan?" |
| Current assets vs current liabilities | **Capital structure** (debt vs equity) and long-term stability |

### 5A. Debt-to-Equity (D/E) Ratio

$$\text{D/E} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}}$$

- **Debt** = total interest-bearing debt **or** total liabilities
- **Equity** = Share capital + Reserves & Surplus (retained earnings)

**Meaning:** How much the company has **borrowed** for every ₹1 of owners' money.

| High D/E | Low D/E |
|---|---|
| More borrowing, heavier interest burden, **financial risk ⬆️** ⚠️ | Runs mostly on owners' money; lower risk ✅ |
| In good times, shareholder returns rise (leverage) | ⚠️ If too low, it isn't using cheaper debt; growth may be slow |

> ⚠️ The slide gives two definitions of "Debt", and they give **different** answers (see Section 6). Use whatever the question specifies. If nothing is specified, **interest-bearing debt** is more common.

### 5B. Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)

$$\text{ICR} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense}}, \quad \text{EBIT = Earnings Before Interest \& Tax}$$

> *"How many times can the company's operating profit cover its interest expense?"*

| High ICR | Low ICR |
|---|---|
| Interest is easily paid; the bank is happy ✅ | A large share of profit goes to interest ⚠️ |
| | **ICR < 1** = operating profit can't even cover interest 🚨 |

> 💡 If only PBT is given, **EBIT = PBT + Interest**. This is a favourite exam trap.

## 6. Solved Example: Sharma Traders 🧮

> (The slides have no numerical example; this is for practice.)

**Balance sheet data (31 March):**

| Current Assets | ₹ | Current Liabilities | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 1,00,000 | Trade payables | 2,00,000 |
| Marketable securities | 50,000 | Short-term borrowings | 1,00,000 |
| Trade receivables | 2,50,000 | Accrued expenses | 60,000 |
| Inventory | 3,00,000 | **Total CL** | **3,60,000** |
| Prepaid expenses | 20,000 | | |
| **Total CA** | **7,20,000** | | |

**Other data:** Long-term loan ₹6,00,000 · Share capital ₹5,00,000 · Reserves ₹3,75,000 · EBIT ₹3,50,000 · Interest ₹70,000
COGS ₹18,25,000 · Avg inventory ₹2,50,000 · Net credit sales ₹21,90,000 · Avg receivables ₹1,80,000 · Credit purchases ₹16,42,500 · Avg payables ₹1,80,000

**Liquidity**

| Ratio | Calculation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 7,20,000 ÷ 3,60,000 | **2.0** |
| Quick Ratio | (7,20,000 − 3,00,000) ÷ 3,60,000 = 4,20,000 ÷ 3,60,000 | **1.17** |
| Cash Ratio | (1,00,000 + 50,000) ÷ 3,60,000 | **0.42** |

**Efficiency**

| Ratio | Calculation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory Turnover | 18,25,000 ÷ 2,50,000 | **7.3 times** |
| Inventory Holding Period | 2,50,000 ÷ 18,25,000 × 365 | **50 days** |
| Receivables Turnover | 21,90,000 ÷ 1,80,000 | **12.17 times** |
| Collection Period (DSO) | 1,80,000 ÷ 21,90,000 × 365 | **30 days** |
| Payables Turnover | 16,42,500 ÷ 1,80,000 | **9.125 times** |
| Payment Period (DPO) | 1,80,000 ÷ 16,42,500 × 365 | **40 days** |
| **Working Capital Cycle** | 50 + 30 − 40 | **40 days** |

**Solvency**

| Ratio | Calculation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| D/E (interest-bearing debt) | (1,00,000 + 6,00,000) ÷ (5,00,000 + 3,75,000) = 7,00,000 ÷ 8,75,000 | **0.80** |
| D/E (total liabilities) | (3,60,000 + 6,00,000) ÷ 8,75,000 = 9,60,000 ÷ 8,75,000 | **1.10** |
| Interest Coverage | 3,50,000 ÷ 70,000 | **5 times** |

**Teacher's verdict 🩺:** Liquidity is fine (CR 2, QR > 1). Stock sells in 50 days, customers pay in 30 days and suppliers give 40 days of credit, so money is tied up for only 40 days. Debt is moderate and interest is covered 5 times. Overall, the company is **healthy**.

## 7. Quick Revision / Formula Sheet 🧠

| Ratio | Formula | What's good? |
|---|---|---|
| Current Ratio | CA ÷ CL | ~2 (depends on industry) |
| Quick Ratio | (CA − Inventory) ÷ CL | ~1 |
| Cash Ratio | (Cash + CE + Mkt Sec) ÷ CL | Higher = safer |
| Inventory Turnover | COGS ÷ Avg Inventory | Higher = goods sell faster |
| Inventory Days (DIO) | Avg Inventory ÷ COGS × 365 | Lower |
| Receivables Turnover | Net Credit Sales ÷ Avg Receivables | Higher |
| Collection Period (DSO) | Avg Receivables ÷ Net Credit Sales × 365 | Lower |
| Payables Turnover | Credit Purchases (or COGS) ÷ Avg Payables | Context |
| Payment Period (DPO) | Avg Payables ÷ Credit Purchases × 365 | Higher = free financing (within limits) |
| Working Capital Cycle | DIO + DSO − DPO | Lower |
| Debt-to-Equity | Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity | Lower = less risk |
| Interest Coverage | EBIT ÷ Interest | Higher = safe; < 1 = danger |

- **Average = (Opening + Closing) ÷ 2**
- **Days = 365 ÷ Turnover**
- **Liquidity = short term, Solvency = long term**
- **EBIT = PBT + Interest**

## 8. MCQs (Mid–Advanced Level) 📝

**Q1.** A company has current assets of ₹9,00,000 (including inventory of ₹3,00,000) and current liabilities of ₹6,00,000. What are the current ratio and quick ratio?
- A) 1.5 and 0.5
- B) 1.0 and 1.5
- C) 2.0 and 1.0
- D) 1.5 and 1.0

> ✅ **Answer: D.** CR = 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5. Quick assets = 9 − 3 = 6, so QR = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0. 📖 Section 3

**Q2.** A company's current ratio is 2:1 (CA ₹10L, CL ₹5L). It pays ₹2L in cash to creditors. What happens to the current ratio?
- A) It stays the same
- B) It increases
- C) It becomes 1:1
- D) It decreases

> ✅ **Answer: B.** CA = 8L and CL = 3L, so CR = 2.67. When a ratio is already above 1, subtracting the same amount from both numerator and denominator **increases** it. 📖 Section 3A

**Q3.** A company has CA of ₹10L (including inventory of ₹4L) and CL of ₹5L. It buys ₹2L of inventory **on credit**. What happens to the current ratio and quick ratio?
- A) Both decrease
- B) CR stays the same, QR decreases
- C) CR decreases, QR stays the same
- D) Both increase

> ✅ **Answer: A.** CR = 12 ÷ 7 = 1.71 (was 2.0). Quick assets stay at 6L but CL rises to 7L, so QR = 0.86 (was 1.2). Both fall. 📖 Section 3

**Q4.** Cash is ₹80,000, cash equivalents ₹20,000, marketable securities ₹50,000, receivables ₹2,00,000 and current liabilities ₹3,00,000. What is the cash ratio?
- A) 0.83
- B) 0.33
- C) 1.17
- D) 0.50

> ✅ **Answer: D.** (80k + 20k + 50k) ÷ 3L = 1.5L ÷ 3L = 0.5. Receivables are **not** included in the cash ratio. 📖 Section 3C

**Q5.** A company has a current ratio of 4.5 but a quick ratio of only 0.6. What is the best interpretation?
- A) The company has very high debt
- B) The company has a lot of cash
- C) Receivables are very high
- D) A large part of current assets is inventory, which may be slow-moving

> ✅ **Answer: D.** A large gap between CR and QR can only come from **inventory**. Don't be impressed by a high CR; the money may be stuck in stock. 📖 Section 3B

**Q6.** COGS is ₹12,00,000, opening inventory ₹1,80,000 and closing inventory ₹2,20,000. What are the inventory turnover and approximate holding period?
- A) 6.67 times, ~55 days
- B) 6 times, ~45 days
- C) 5.45 times, ~67 days
- D) 6 times, ~61 days

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Avg inventory = (1.8 + 2.2) ÷ 2 = 2L. ITR = 12 ÷ 2 = 6. Days = 365 ÷ 6 = 60.8 ≈ 61. Option C uses closing inventory instead of the average. 📖 Section 4A

**Q7.** An analyst uses **Sales** instead of COGS to calculate inventory turnover. What is the effect on the result?
- A) The ratio becomes negative
- B) The ratio is overstated (looks higher)
- C) No difference
- D) The ratio is understated

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Sales > COGS (because of the profit margin), so the numerator is bigger and the ratio comes out higher. Inventory is held at cost, so COGS is correct. 📖 Section 4A

**Q8.** Net credit sales are ₹36,50,000 and average trade receivables are ₹4,00,000. What is the DSO?
- A) 36.5 days
- B) 91 days
- C) 40 days
- D) 9.1 days

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Credit sales per day = 36.5L ÷ 365 = ₹10,000. DSO = 4,00,000 ÷ 10,000 = 40 days. (Turnover = 9.125, and 365 ÷ 9.125 = 40 ✓) 📖 Section 4B

**Q9.** A company's credit terms are "payment within 30 days", but its DSO is 55 days. What does this indicate?
- A) Inventory is slow-moving
- B) Suppliers are being paid early
- C) Customers are paying late, and collection/credit control is weak
- D) Collection is efficient

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Customers take 25 days longer than the terms allow. This ties up cash and raises the risk of bad debts. 📖 Section 4B

**Q10.** Credit purchases are ₹18,25,000 and average trade payables are ₹3,00,000. What is the average payment period?
- A) 6.08 days
- B) 60 days
- C) 45 days
- D) 73 days

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Purchases per day = 18.25L ÷ 365 = ₹5,000. DPO = 3,00,000 ÷ 5,000 = 60 days. 📖 Section 4C

**Q11.** A company's **credit purchases data is not available**. According to the slide, what can be used to compute payables turnover?
- A) COGS
- B) EBIT
- C) Closing inventory
- D) Net Sales

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Per the slide, COGS can be used when credit purchases are unavailable, since both are at cost level. 📖 Section 4C

**Q12.** The inventory holding period is 45 days, receivable days 35 days and payable days 50 days. Using the slide's formula, what is the working capital cycle?
- A) 30 days
- B) 80 days
- C) 130 days
- D) 60 days

> ✅ **Answer: A.** 45 + 35 − 50 = 30 days. (DIO + DSO = 80 days is the gross operating cycle.) 📖 Section 4D

**Q13.** An online retailer has DIO of 20 days, DSO of 2 days and DPO of 40 days. What does this mean?
- A) The company is insolvent
- B) Cycle is +62 days, so it needs a lot of working capital
- C) Cycle is −18 days, meaning suppliers' money is funding operations
- D) Cycle is 0, so there is no effect

> ✅ **Answer: C.** 20 + 2 − 40 = −18. Cash arrives from customers 18 days before suppliers must be paid. A negative cycle is **good** for working capital. 📖 Section 4D

**Q14.** Which of the following steps will **shorten** the working capital cycle?
- A) Negotiating 45 days of credit from suppliers instead of 30
- B) Giving customers 60 days of credit instead of 30
- C) Buying and holding extra inventory in bulk
- D) Paying suppliers early

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Higher DPO shortens the cycle. B raises DSO, C raises DIO and D lowers DPO; all three **lengthen** the cycle. 📖 Section 4D

**Q15.** Total debt is ₹12L, share capital ₹5L and reserves & surplus ₹3L. What is the D/E ratio?
- A) 4.0
- B) 2.4
- C) 0.67
- D) 1.5

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Equity = 5 + 3 = 8L. D/E = 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5. Option B uses only share capital and forgets reserves. 📖 Section 5A

**Q16.** A company has interest-bearing debt of ₹6L, total liabilities of ₹10L and equity of ₹8L. Which statement is correct?
- A) D/E is 0.75 under both definitions
- B) D/E = 0.75 using interest-bearing debt and 1.25 using total liabilities
- C) D/E = 2.25
- D) D/E = 0.75 using total liabilities

> ✅ **Answer: B.** 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75 and 10 ÷ 8 = 1.25. That's why you must use the **same definition** when comparing. 📖 Section 5A

**Q17.** PBT is ₹6,00,000 and interest expense is ₹2,00,000. What is the interest coverage ratio?
- A) 4 times
- B) 3 times
- C) 2 times
- D) 0.33 times

> ✅ **Answer: A.** EBIT = PBT + Interest = 6L + 2L = 8L. ICR = 8 ÷ 2 = 4. Trap: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 is wrong, because interest has already been deducted from PBT. 📖 Section 5B

**Q18.** A company's interest coverage ratio is 0.8. What does this mean?
- A) Operating profit is less than interest expense, meaning high solvency risk
- B) The company's current ratio is 0.8
- C) The company can easily pay its interest
- D) The company has no debt

> ✅ **Answer: A.** ICR < 1 means EBIT cannot cover even the full interest. This is a strong warning sign of default. 📖 Section 5B

**Q19.** Before giving a company a **7-year term loan**, which ratios will a bank focus on most?
- A) Cash ratio and DSO
- B) D/E ratio and interest coverage ratio
- C) Inventory turnover and DPO
- D) Only the current ratio

> ✅ **Answer: B.** A long-term loan requires checking **solvency**: how much debt there is (D/E) and the capacity to pay interest (ICR). Liquidity ratios are for the short term. 📖 Section 5

**Q20.** In the same industry, Company A has an inventory turnover of 12 and Company B has 4. What are their approximate inventory holding periods?
- A) Both ~60 days
- B) A: ~91 days, B: ~30 days
- C) A: ~30 days, B: ~91 days
- D) A: ~12 days, B: ~4 days

> ✅ **Answer: C.** 365 ÷ 12 ≈ 30 and 365 ÷ 4 ≈ 91. A's goods sell three times faster, so A is better at inventory management. 📖 Section 4A

**Q21.** In which case can a **very high current ratio** (e.g. 6:1) be a negative signal?
- A) When D/E is low
- B) When ICR is high
- C) When a lot of cash is idle or there is a pile of unsold stock, i.e. resources aren't used productively
- D) Never; high is always good

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Liquidity is good, but **efficiency** is poor: the money isn't working. Always read ratios together, not in isolation. 📖 Sections 3A & 4
