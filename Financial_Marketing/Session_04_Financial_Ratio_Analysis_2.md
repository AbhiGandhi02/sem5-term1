## 1. Financial Ratios: Quick Recap

**Financial ratios** are numbers derived from financial statements, used to judge a company's **financial health** and **performance**. They allow two kinds of comparison:
- **Over time** (this year vs last year)
- **Against industry peers** (TCS vs Infosys)

**Objectives (in short):**

| Objective | Question |
|---|---|
| Profitability | Is enough profit being made for owners? |
| Liquidity | Can short-term bills be paid? |
| Operational efficiency | Are assets being used well? |
| Solvency & risk | Can long-term debts be paid? |
| Return on capital | What return did owners + lenders get on their money? |
| Comparisons | Against time, competitors, industry and **budget** |
| Decision-making | Data for management |

### 5 Categories of Ratios

| Category | One-line question |
|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | Can short-term obligations be met? |
| **Efficiency** | How efficiently are assets and working capital used? |
| **Solvency / Leverage** | How much long-term obligation and financial risk is there? |
| **Profitability & Return** ⭐ (today) | How well are profits and returns being generated? |
| **Market / Investor** ⭐ (today) | How does the company look from the shareholders' and stock market's view? |

## 2. Profitability & Return Ratios: The Big Picture

> *"How well is the company converting its revenue and invested resources into profit and returns?"*

**Looking only at the profit number can mislead you.** A company can have:
- Strong liquidity but weak profitability
- Very high sales but very low **margins**
- High profit but low **return on capital** (too much money invested)
- High returns, but only because of **excessive borrowing (leverage)**, which is risky

> 💡 So always compare profit against a base: **sales, assets or capital**.

There are two groups:
- **Profit Margin Ratios** → profit ÷ **revenue**
- **Return Ratios** → profit ÷ **capital / assets / equity**

### The profit ladder (income statement flow)

```
Revenue
 − Cost of Sales (COGS)
 = Gross Profit                         → GPM
 − Operating expenses (salary, rent, marketing, depreciation/amortisation)
 = EBIT (Operating Profit)              → OPM, ROCE
 − Interest (finance cost)
 = PBT (Profit Before Tax)
 − Tax
 = PAT (Profit After Tax / Net Profit)  → NPM, ROA, ROE, EPS
```

> 💡 **In simple words:** Ye seedhi yaad kar lo. Har ratio ka numerator isi seedhi ke kisi ek step se aata hai.

## 3. Profit Margin Ratios

### 3.1 Gross Profit Margin (GPM)

$$\text{GPM} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100 = \frac{\text{Revenue − Cost of Sales}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100$$

- **Meaning:** How much is left from every ₹100 of sales after COGS. It shows the profitability of the **basic production/purchasing activity**, **before** operating expenses, interest and tax.
- **High GPM:** strong pricing power or cheap raw materials (e.g. branded companies).
- **Low/falling GPM:** input costs are rising, or competition is forcing price cuts.

### 3.2 Operating Profit Margin (OPM)

$$\text{OPM} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100$$

- **EBIT** = Earnings Before Interest & Tax, i.e. profit **after** operating expenses and depreciation/amortisation but **before** interest and tax.
- **Meaning:** How profitable the core business operations are. Financing decisions (whether a loan was taken) don't affect it.
- **High OPM:** efficient operations, overheads under control.
- **Low OPM:** high expenses such as salaries, rent, marketing.

> ⚠️ The slide says OPM is "Also called EBIT". In fact **EBIT is an amount** (operating profit) and **OPM is a percentage** (EBIT ÷ Revenue).

### 3.3 Net Profit Margin (NPM)

$$\text{NPM} = \frac{\text{Profit After Tax}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100$$

- **Meaning:** Profit left from every ₹100 of sales after **all** expenses (operating, finance cost, tax).
- **High NPM:** strong overall cost control.
- **Low NPM but healthy OPM:** interest (high debt) or tax is eating the profit.

> 💡 **In simple words:** Teeno ko saath padho. GPM achha, OPM kharab → operating kharche zyada hain. OPM achha, NPM kharab → interest/debt ki problem hai.

## 4. Return Ratios

### 4.1 Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

$$\text{ROCE} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Capital Employed}} \times 100$$

$$\text{Capital Employed} = \text{Total Assets − Current Liabilities} \quad \text{OR} \quad \text{Equity + Long-term Debt}$$

- **Meaning:** How much operating profit was made from **long-term capital** (both owners' and lenders' money).
- The numerator is **EBIT** because capital includes lenders' money too. Interest is the lenders' return, so we take profit **before** interest.
- **High ROCE:** capital is used efficiently. If ROCE > borrowing cost, borrowing to expand can be worthwhile.
- **Low ROCE:** a lot of capital invested but little operating profit.

> 💡 **In simple words:** Dono formule same answer kyun dete hain? Balance sheet se: Total Assets = Current Liabilities + Long-term Debt + Equity. Isliye Total Assets − CL = Equity + LT Debt.

### 4.2 Return on Assets (ROA)

$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Profit After Tax}}{\text{Average Total Assets}} \times 100, \qquad \text{Average Assets} = \frac{\text{Opening + Closing Assets}}{2}$$

- **Meaning:** How much net profit the company makes from its **entire asset base**.
- **Why average?** Profit is earned over the whole year, while assets keep changing during the year. Averaging the start and end of the year makes the comparison fair.
- **High ROA:** very productive assets (often high in asset-light businesses like software).
- **Low ROA:** heavy assets but low profit (e.g. steel, telecom).

### 4.3 Return on Equity (ROE)

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Profit After Tax}}{\text{Average Shareholders' Equity}} \times 100$$

**Shareholders' Equity = Share Capital + Retained Earnings** (retained earnings = undistributed profit that belongs to shareholders).

- **Meaning:** Net profit made on every ₹100 invested by shareholders. This is **the owners' favourite ratio**.
- **High ROE:** good return on owners' money. But **check it isn't only due to high debt** (leverage inflates ROE artificially).
- **Low ROE:** shareholders' money isn't earning a good return.

> 💡 **DuPont trick (advanced):** ROE = NPM × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
> = (PAT/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity).
> **In simple words:** Isse pata chalta hai ki ROE high hone ki wajah margin hai, efficiency hai, ya **leverage**. Equity multiplier bahut zyada hai to ROE debt se aa raha hai, aur ye risky hai.

### 4.4 Return on Investment (ROI)

$$\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{Gain from Investment − Investment Cost}}{\text{Investment Cost}} \times 100$$

- **Meaning:** The return on a **particular investment** (a project, an ad campaign, a machine, shares).
- Unlike ROE/ROCE, there is **no single universal accounting formula**. Each company defines "gain" for its own needs.
- **Example:** A ₹2,00,000 ad campaign produced a ₹2,50,000 gain → ROI = 50,000 / 2,00,000 = **25%**.

### Return ratios at a glance

| Ratio | Numerator | Denominator | Whose perspective? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ROCE | EBIT | Capital Employed | Owners + Lenders |
| ROA | PAT | Average Total Assets | Whole asset base |
| ROE | PAT | Average Shareholders' Equity | Owners only |
| ROI | Gain − Cost | Investment Cost | One specific investment |

## 5. Market / Investor Ratios

> *"How much does the stock market value the company's earnings, assets and growth?"* or *"How much value is the company creating for shareholders?"*

These ratios use stock market data: **market price per share, market cap, EPS, DPS, book value per share**. Investors use them to judge whether a share is **undervalued or overvalued**, how much is earned per share, and what dividends/growth to expect.

### 5.1 Earnings Per Share (EPS)

$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{PAT − Preference Dividend}}{\text{Number of Ordinary Shares}} = \frac{\text{Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders}}{\text{Number of Ordinary Shares}}$$

- **Meaning:** How much profit belongs to each ordinary (equity) share.
- **Why subtract preference dividend?** Preference shareholders get their fixed dividend first. What remains belongs to ordinary shareholders.
- **Rising EPS:** profit per share is growing, a good sign. (Note: issuing new shares can **dilute** EPS.)

### 5.2 Price-Earnings Ratio (P/E)

$$\text{P/E} = \frac{\text{Market Price Per Share}}{\text{EPS}}$$

- **Meaning:** How many rupees investors are willing to pay for ₹1 of current earnings.
- **High P/E:** the market expects strong future growth, **or** the share is overvalued.
- **Low P/E:** low growth expectations, higher risk, **or** the share is undervalued (a value-buying opportunity).
- Always compare P/E with companies in the **same industry**.

### 5.3 Dividend Per Share (DPS)

$$\text{DPS} = \frac{\text{Total Ordinary Dividends}}{\text{Number of Ordinary Shares}}$$

- **Meaning:** Dividend received per share.

> ⚠️ The slide heading says "Dividend Per Share **(EPS)**". That's a typo; the correct abbreviation is **DPS**.

### 5.4 Dividend Payout Ratio

$$\text{Payout} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}} \times 100 = \frac{\text{Total Dividends}}{\text{Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders}} \times 100$$

- **Meaning:** What share of earnings was distributed as dividends.
- **Retention Ratio = 100% − Payout.** This is the part reinvested in the business (retained earnings).
- **High payout:** a mature company with few growth opportunities (e.g. ITC, Coal India type). A payout above 100% means the company is distributing more than it earns, which isn't sustainable long term.
- **Low payout:** a growth company reinvesting in expansion.

### 5.5 Dividend Yield

$$\text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{Market Price Per Share}} \times 100$$

- **Meaning:** If you buy the share at today's price, the yearly % return from dividends alone.
- **High yield:** good for income-seeking investors. But sometimes it's high only because the price has fallen, so check the reason.

### 5.6 Market Capitalisation

$$\text{Market Cap} = \text{Market Price Per Share} \times \text{Number of Shares Outstanding}$$

- Technically **not** a ratio, but it shows the **total market value of the company's equity**. It is always read alongside investor ratios.

### 5.7 Enterprise Value (EV)

$$\text{EV} = \text{Market Cap} + \text{Total Debt} − \text{Cash \& Cash Equivalents}$$

- **Meaning:** The value of the **whole business**, including both shareholders' money and debt, minus cash.
- **Why subtract cash?** If you buy the whole company, its cash becomes yours, which effectively lowers your cost.
- **Uses:** business valuation, **mergers & acquisitions**, investment analysis.
- If cash > debt (a net cash company), **EV < Market Cap**.

> ⚠️ The slide mistakenly **repeats the Market Cap formula** under EV. The correct EV formula is given above: **Market Cap + Debt − Cash**.

## 6. Solved Example: All Ratios on One Company 🧮

**Sunrise Foods Ltd** (₹ lakh, FY 2025-26):

| Income Statement | ₹ lakh | | Balance Sheet / Market Data | Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 50.0 | | Total assets: opening / closing | 36 / 44 |
| COGS | 30.0 | | Current liabilities (closing) | 4.0 |
| Operating exp. (incl. depreciation) | 12.0 | | Long-term debt (closing) | 13.8 |
| Interest | 2.0 | | Equity: opening / closing | 23.8 / 26.2 |
| Tax (25% of PBT) | 1.5 | | Preference dividend | 0.5 |
| | | | Ordinary dividend | 1.6 |
| | | | Ordinary shares | 1,00,000 |
| | | | Market price per share | ₹60 |
| | | | Cash | 6.0 |

**Step 1: The profit ladder**
Gross Profit = 50 − 30 = **20** → EBIT = 20 − 12 = **8** → PBT = 8 − 2 = **6** → PAT = 6 − 1.5 = **4.5**

**Step 2: Ratios**

| Ratio | Calculation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| GPM | 20 / 50 × 100 | **40%** |
| OPM | 8 / 50 × 100 | **16%** |
| NPM | 4.5 / 50 × 100 | **9%** |
| Capital Employed | 44 − 4 = 40 (check: 26.2 + 13.8 = 40 ✅) | ₹40 L |
| ROCE | 8 / 40 × 100 | **20%** |
| ROA | 4.5 / ((36 + 44)/2 = 40) × 100 | **11.25%** |
| ROE | 4.5 / ((23.8 + 26.2)/2 = 25) × 100 | **18%** |
| EPS | (4.5 − 0.5) L / 1,00,000 = 4,00,000 / 1,00,000 | **₹4** |
| P/E | 60 / 4 | **15×** |
| DPS | 1,60,000 / 1,00,000 | **₹1.60** |
| Payout | 1.6 / 4 × 100 | **40%** (Retention 60%) |
| Dividend Yield | 1.6 / 60 × 100 | **2.67%** |
| Market Cap | 60 × 1,00,000 | **₹60 L** |
| EV | 60 + 13.8 − 6 | **₹67.8 L** |

**DuPont check:** NPM 9% × Asset turnover (50/40 = 1.25) × Equity multiplier (40/25 = 1.6) = 0.09 × 1.25 × 1.6 = **18%** ✅ (matches ROE)

**Teacher's interpretation 👨‍🏫:**
- GPM is 40% but NPM only 9%. The operating expenses in between (12L) are the biggest burden.
- ROE (18%) > ROA (11.25%) because the company uses debt (leverage). ROCE is 20%; if the loan interest rate is lower than this, borrowing is beneficial.
- P/E is 15, so investors pay ₹15 for every ₹1 of earnings. This must be compared with peers' P/E.

## 7. Quick Revision / Formula Sheet 🧠

| Ratio | Formula | One line |
|---|---|---|
| GPM | (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue × 100 | Basic product profitability |
| OPM | EBIT / Revenue × 100 | Core operations profitability |
| NPM | PAT / Revenue × 100 | Profit after all expenses |
| ROCE | EBIT / (Total Assets − CL) × 100 | Return on long-term capital |
| ROA | PAT / Avg Total Assets × 100 | How productive assets are |
| ROE | PAT / Avg Shareholders' Equity × 100 | Owners' return |
| ROI | (Gain − Cost) / Cost × 100 | Return on one investment |
| EPS | (PAT − Pref Div) / No. of ordinary shares | Profit per share |
| P/E | Price / EPS | What the market pays for ₹1 of earnings |
| DPS | Ordinary dividends / No. of shares | Dividend per share |
| Payout | DPS / EPS × 100 | % of earnings distributed |
| Div. Yield | DPS / Price × 100 | % return from dividends |
| Market Cap | Price × Shares outstanding | Market value of equity |
| EV | Market Cap + Debt − Cash | Value of the whole business |

**Memory tricks:**
- **EBIT** ratios (OPM, ROCE): concern both owners + lenders.
- **PAT** ratios (NPM, ROA, ROE, EPS): concern only the owners' share.
- For Flow ÷ Stock (profit ÷ assets/equity), use the **average** stock.

## 8. MCQs (Mid–Advanced Level) 📝

**Q1.** A company has revenue of ₹80 lakh and cost of sales of ₹52 lakh. What is its GPM?
- A) 35%
- B) 28%
- C) 65%
- D) 53.8%

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Gross Profit = 80 − 52 = 28L. GPM = 28/80 × 100 = **35%**. (65% is the cost ratio; 53.8% = 28/52 uses the wrong denominator.) 📖 Section 3.1

**Q2.** If Gross Profit is ₹18 lakh and GPM is 30%, what are Revenue and COGS?
- A) Revenue ₹60L, COGS ₹18L
- B) Revenue ₹24L, COGS ₹6L
- C) Revenue ₹60L, COGS ₹42L
- D) Revenue ₹54L, COGS ₹36L

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Revenue = 18 / 0.30 = **₹60L**. COGS = 60 − 18 = **₹42L**. 📖 Section 3.1

**Q3.** A company takes a large new loan, sharply increasing its interest cost. Everything else stays the same. Which margin will fall?
- A) Only NPM
- B) Both GPM and OPM
- C) All three
- D) Only GPM

> ✅ **Answer: A.** GPM and OPM are based on profit **before** interest. Interest only reduces PBT/PAT, so only NPM falls. 📖 Section 2 (profit ladder)

**Q4.** Company A has a GPM of 45% but an OPM of only 5%. What is the most accurate interpretation?
- A) The tax rate is very high
- B) Raw materials are very expensive
- C) Interest cost is very high
- D) Operating expenses (salary, rent, marketing, depreciation) are very high

> ✅ **Answer: D.** The gap between GPM and OPM consists only of **operating expenses**. Interest and tax come after OPM. 📖 Section 3

**Q5.** Total assets are ₹90L, current liabilities ₹15L and EBIT ₹15L. What is ROCE?
- A) 20%
- B) 16.67%
- C) 15%
- D) 25%

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Capital Employed = 90 − 15 = 75L. ROCE = 15/75 × 100 = **20%**. (16.67% = 15/90, i.e. forgetting to subtract CL.) 📖 Section 4.1

**Q6.** Why is EBIT, rather than PAT, used as the numerator of ROCE?
- A) Because standards say EBIT must always be used
- B) Because capital employed includes lenders' money, and interest is the lenders' return, so using profit before interest is consistent
- C) Because EBIT is always larger
- D) Because tax has no effect

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Numerator and denominator must relate to the same group. Capital employed = Equity + LT Debt (owners + lenders), so use the profit available to **both**, i.e. EBIT. 📖 Section 4.1

**Q7.** PAT is ₹6L, opening total assets ₹45L and closing ₹55L. What is ROA?
- A) 10.91%
- B) 13.33%
- C) 12%
- D) 11%

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Average assets = (45 + 55)/2 = 50L. ROA = 6/50 × 100 = **12%**. 📖 Section 4.2

**Q8.** PAT is ₹9L, opening equity ₹40L and closing ₹50L. What is ROE?
- A) 15%
- B) 22.5%
- C) 20%
- D) 18%

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Average equity = (40 + 50)/2 = 45L. ROE = 9/45 × 100 = **20%**. (22.5% uses opening equity, 18% uses closing.) 📖 Section 4.3

**Q9.** Company X has an ROE of 28% but an ROA of only 6%. What is the most likely reason?
- A) The company's tax rate is low
- B) The company uses a lot of debt (leverage)
- C) The company holds a lot of cash
- D) The company's margins are very high

> ✅ **Answer: B.** ROE/ROA = Assets/Equity (equity multiplier) ≈ 4.7×, so a large part of the assets is financed by debt. This makes ROE look high but also raises risk. This is the slide's point about "high returns because of excessive leverage". 📖 Section 4.3 DuPont

**Q10.** In a DuPont analysis, NPM is 10%, Asset Turnover 1.5× and Equity Multiplier 2×. What is ROE?
- A) 15%
- B) 20%
- C) 30%
- D) 13.5%

> ✅ **Answer: C.** ROE = 0.10 × 1.5 × 2 = 0.30 = **30%**. 📖 Section 4.3

**Q11.** You bought shares for ₹1,20,000 and sold them for ₹1,50,000. What is the ROI?
- A) 25%
- B) 125%
- C) 20%
- D) 30%

> ✅ **Answer: A.** ROI = (1,50,000 − 1,20,000) / 1,20,000 × 100 = 30,000 / 1,20,000 = **25%**. (20% comes from dividing by the selling price.) 📖 Section 4.4

**Q12.** PAT is ₹25L, preference dividend ₹5L, and there are 4,00,000 ordinary shares. What is EPS?
- A) ₹6.25
- B) ₹5.00
- C) ₹4.00
- D) ₹7.50

> ✅ **Answer: B.** EPS = (25L − 5L) / 4L shares = 20,00,000 / 4,00,000 = **₹5**. (₹6.25 = forgetting to subtract the preference dividend.) 📖 Section 5.1

**Q13.** A share has EPS of ₹8 and a P/E of 20×. If DPS is ₹2, what are the market price and dividend yield?
- A) ₹160; 1.25%
- B) ₹2.5; 80%
- C) ₹160; 25%
- D) ₹28; 7.1%

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Price = P/E × EPS = 20 × 8 = **₹160**. Yield = 2/160 × 100 = **1.25%**. (25% is the payout, not the yield.) 📖 Sections 5.2, 5.5

**Q14.** EPS is ₹12 and DPS is ₹3. What are the dividend payout and retention ratios?
- A) Payout 75%, Retention 25%
- B) Payout 25%, Retention 25%
- C) Payout 400%, Retention −300%
- D) Payout 25%, Retention 75%

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Payout = 3/12 × 100 = **25%**. Retention = 100 − 25 = **75%**. 📖 Section 5.4

**Q15.** A company's dividend payout ratio is 130%. What does this mean?
- A) The company paid no dividend
- B) The company is paying more dividend than its current earnings (from past reserves), which isn't sustainable long term
- C) The retention ratio is 130%
- D) The company is growing very fast

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Payout > 100% means DPS > EPS. The extra money comes from retained earnings or borrowing, and the retention ratio becomes −30%. 📖 Section 5.4

**Q16.** Of two companies in the same industry, Company P has a P/E of 45× and Company Q 12×. What is the most balanced interpretation?
- A) P/E has nothing to do with growth
- B) The market expects more growth from P (or P is overvalued) and less from Q (or Q is undervalued); more analysis is needed to decide
- C) P is always the better investment
- D) Q is always the better investment

> ✅ **Answer: B.** A high P/E means the market pays a premium for future earnings. That can reflect growth expectations or overvaluation. P/E alone doesn't give a final decision. 📖 Section 5.2

**Q17.** A company has 2 crore shares outstanding at ₹250 each. Total debt is ₹120 crore and cash ₹40 crore. What are the market cap and EV?
- A) Market Cap ₹500 Cr; EV ₹660 Cr
- B) Market Cap ₹500 Cr; EV ₹420 Cr
- C) Market Cap ₹250 Cr; EV ₹330 Cr
- D) Market Cap ₹500 Cr; EV ₹580 Cr

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Market Cap = 250 × 2 Cr = **₹500 Cr**. EV = 500 + 120 − 40 = **₹580 Cr**. (₹660 Cr wrongly adds cash.) 📖 Sections 5.6, 5.7

**Q18.** In which situation will Enterprise Value be **lower** than Market Cap?
- A) When the share price falls
- B) When the company's debt exceeds its cash
- C) When the company has more cash than debt (a net cash company)
- D) Never

> ✅ **Answer: C.** EV = Market Cap + (Debt − Cash). If Cash > Debt, the bracket is negative and EV < Market Cap. 📖 Section 5.7

**Q19.** Why is **average** total assets used in ROA instead of closing assets?
- A) Because closing assets are always wrong
- B) Because profit is earned over the whole year (a flow), while assets are a balance at one date (a stock) that changes during the year
- C) To make the calculation easier
- D) Because tax is charged on average assets

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Averaging opening and closing balances fairly matches a flow (the year's profit) with a stock (a balance at a date). The same logic applies to ROE. 📖 Section 4.2

**Q20.** A retailer's sales grow 30% every year, but its NPM has fallen from 1% to 0.3%. According to the slides, what is the key lesson?
- A) Sales growth is everything
- B) The company is certainly in a liquidity crisis
- C) NPM has no relation to sales
- D) High sales don't mean high profitability, so profit should be judged relative to sales (margin)

> ✅ **Answer: D.** This is the slide's point about "High sales but low profit margins". Always judge profit relative to sales/assets/capital. 📖 Section 2

**Q21.** Equity is ₹50L and long-term debt ₹30L. EBIT is ₹16L, interest ₹3L and tax ₹3.25L. What are ROCE and PAT?
- A) ROCE 20%; PAT ₹9.75L
- B) ROCE 16.25%; PAT ₹9.75L
- C) ROCE 20%; PAT ₹13L
- D) ROCE 32%; PAT ₹9.75L

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Capital employed = 50 + 30 = 80L. ROCE = 16/80 × 100 = **20%**. PAT = 16 − 3 − 3.25 = **₹9.75L**. (32% divides by equity only.) 📖 Section 4.1

**Q22.** Which pair is **incorrectly** matched?
- A) OPM → EBIT / Revenue
- B) Dividend Yield → DPS / Market Price
- C) ROE → PAT / Average Shareholders' Equity
- D) ROCE → PAT / Capital Employed

> ✅ **Answer: D.** The numerator of ROCE is **EBIT**, not PAT. 📖 Section 7
