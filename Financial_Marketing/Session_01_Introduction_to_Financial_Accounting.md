## 1. What is Accounting?

**AICPA definition:** Accounting is the *art* of **recording**, **classifying** and **summarizing** financial transactions (in terms of money), and then **interpreting** the results.

> 💡 **In simple words:** Business mein jo bhi paise ka len-den hota hai, use likho → group karo → total nikalo → samjho ki matlab kya hai.

| Step | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Recording | Write down every transaction | "Sold goods worth ₹5,000 today" |
| Classifying | Group similar items together | All sales in one place, all expenses in another |
| Summarizing | Prepare the final statements | P&L, Balance Sheet |
| Interpreting | Understand what the numbers mean | "Profit is falling, cut costs" |

- Accounting is called the **"Language of Business"** because every business tells its story through numbers.
- Every business activity (buying inventory, paying salaries, collecting from customers, borrowing) has a **financial impact**. Accounting captures that impact and turns it into **useful information for decision-making**.

> 📌 Warren Buffett's point, in short: if you won't learn to read financial statements, don't pick stocks yourself.

## 2. Objectives of Accounting (why do we need it?)

Every business needs answers: Are we profitable? How much cash do we have? How much do customers owe us? What must we pay? Is the business healthy? Should we expand or cut costs? **Accounting answers all of this with reliable data.**

| Objective | Simple meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Systematic records** | Record every transaction in an organized way | A grocery store records daily sales, purchases and expenses |
| **Determine profit/loss** | Revenue − Expenses | Did we earn or lose money this year? |
| **Show financial position** | What you **own** (assets) and what you **owe** (liabilities) | In India, usually measured on **31 March** |
| **Decision-making** | Give management the data | Open a new store? Buy a new plant? |
| **Safeguard assets** | Catch errors, misuse, theft | Count inventory/cash to find shortages |
| **Legal compliance** | Records for tax, audit, regulators | GST returns, financial statements |
| **Inform stakeholders** | Owners, investors, banks, govt | A bank reviews audited statements before giving a loan |

### 🛒 The retail store example
A store has money coming in every day (sales) and going out (suppliers, salaries, rent, electricity). At month-end, the bank balance has gone up. **But is that profit?** Not necessarily!

> 💡 **In simple words:** Bank balance badhna ≠ profit. Ho sakta hai loan aaya ho, ya supplier ko abhi payment nahi kiya ho. Isliye proper accounting chahiye.

Accounting tells you the total sales, total expenses, the actual profit/loss, and how much stock is left. It also gives extra insight: which product is most profitable, whether ads are paying off, whether shipping costs are too high, and how much cash is available for growth.

**Without accounting, these decisions would be pure guesswork.**

> 🎯 **Primary objective:** Provide accurate and reliable financial information so the business can measure performance, assess financial health and make better decisions.

## 3. Users of Accounting (who uses it?)

Accounting is not just for accountants. There are two types of users:

### A. Internal Users (people **inside** the company)

| User | What they use it for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Management** | Checking profitability, budgets/forecasts, cost control, expansion decisions | A retail chain finds its most profitable stores and opens new branches nearby |
| **Employees** | Job security, chances of a raise/bonus, long-term stability of the company | If the company reports strong profits, employees expect better incentives |

### B. External Users (people **outside** the company)

| User | What they use it for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Shareholders & Investors** | Measuring performance & profit, tracking growth, checking **ROI**, comparing investments | Reviewing annual, quarterly and half-yearly results to see if the company is creating value |
| **Lenders / Banks** | **Creditworthiness**, lending decisions, **liquidity & solvency**, debt levels | A bank approves a corporate loan only after reviewing audited statements |
| **Suppliers & Creditors** | Financial stability, setting **credit limits**, payment capability | A supplier checks the financial position before extending more credit |
| **Government & Regulators** | Verifying taxes, compliance with accounting standards, regulating business | Tax authorities verify GST and income tax filings |

> 💡 **In simple words:** **Liquidity** = short-term bills time pe chukaa sakte ho ya nahi. **Solvency** = long-term mein saare debts chukaa sakte ho ya nahi.

> ⚠️ The slides for shareholders, lenders, suppliers and government are wrongly titled "Internal Users". All of these are **External Users**.

## 4. Types of Accounting

| Type | Purpose | Main Users | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Financial Accounting** | Record transactions and prepare statements for reporting | Investors, lenders, shareholders, regulators, govt | Balance Sheet, P&L, Cash Flow Statement |
| **Management Accounting** | Planning, decision-making, performance evaluation. **Future-oriented** | Managers, executives | Budgets, forecasts, profitability analysis |
| **Cost Accounting** | Find and control the **true cost** of products/services/operations | Management | Material, labour, overhead costs |
| **Tax Accounting** | Calculate the correct tax and follow tax laws | Tax authorities, management | Taxable income, deductions, filings |
| **Auditing** | **Independent** examination of records to check accuracy | Shareholders, investors, regulators, management | Verification, compliance, internal controls |
| **Forensic Accounting** | **Investigating** fraud and financial irregularities | Courts, investigators, management | Accounting evidence |

> 💡 **Key difference:** Financial accounting follows **accounting standards**; tax accounting follows **tax laws**. That's why "book profit" and "taxable profit" can differ.
> Financial accounting = report card of the past (for outsiders). Management accounting = plan for the future (for insiders).

## 5. Accounting Concepts & Conventions

These are the **basic rules** that guide how transactions are recorded, classified, measured and reported. They make statements **Consistent, Comparable, Reliable, Understandable** and **Useful for decisions**.

### 5A. Accounting Concepts (9)

**1. Business Entity Concept:** The business and its owner are **separate**.
> Jeff Bezos owns Amazon, but his personal expenses don't go into Amazon's books. Money the owner takes out of the business is **drawings**, not a business expense.

**2. Going Concern Concept:** We assume the business **will keep running** for the foreseeable future, not shut down tomorrow.
> That's why a machine is shown at cost and depreciated over years, not at "what it would fetch if sold today".

**3. Money Measurement Concept:** Only things that can be **reliably measured in money** are recorded.
> Employee morale, CEO quality, the "feel" of a brand: none of these go in the books.

**4. Accounting Period Concept:** A business runs for years, but performance is measured over **fixed periods**: monthly, quarterly, half-yearly, annually.
> FY for most Indian companies: **1 April 2026 – 31 March 2027**.

**5. Historical Cost Concept:** Record assets at their **acquisition cost**, not current market value.
> Land bought for ₹50L in 2015 is worth ₹1.2 Cr today, but it is initially recorded at ₹50L.

**6. Dual Aspect Concept:** Every transaction has **at least two effects**. This is the foundation of **double-entry bookkeeping**.
> `Assets = Liabilities + Equity`. Take a ₹4L loan → Cash (asset) +4L and Loan (liability) +4L.

**7. Accrual Concept:** Record a transaction when the **economic activity happens**, not when cash moves.
> ABC Ltd ran a ₹1,00,000 ad campaign in **March 2026** and paid in **April**. The expense belongs to **March**, because the service was received in March.

**8. Matching Concept:** Show an expense in the **same period** as the revenue it helped generate. *"Match the cost with its revenue."*

> **FashionHub example:** Bought 1,000 shirts @ ₹1,000 (₹10,00,000). Sold 600 shirts @ ₹1,800 (₹10,80,000).
>
> | | Calculation | Amount |
> |---|---|---|
> | Sales | 600 × ₹1,800 | ₹10,80,000 |
> | Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) | 600 × ₹1,000 | ₹6,00,000 |
> | **Gross Profit** | 10,80,000 − 6,00,000 | **₹4,80,000** |
> | Closing Inventory (asset) | 400 × ₹1,000 | ₹4,00,000 |
>
> ❌ Wrong way: 10,80,000 − 10,00,000 = ₹80,000. The 400 unsold shirts are still stock (an asset), not an expense!

**9. Realisation / Revenue Recognition Concept:** Recognise revenue when it is **earned**, i.e. when the **performance obligation is satisfied**, not when cash is received.

> **Netflix example:** You paid ₹12,000 upfront on 1 April 2025 for 12 months. Netflix can't treat the full ₹12,000 as revenue now, because it still owes you 12 months of service. It recognises **₹1,000** of revenue each month.

> 💡 **In simple words:** Accrual, Matching, Realisation teeno ek hi family ke hain: **"Kab hua" matter karta hai, "paisa kab aaya" nahi.**

### 5B. Accounting Conventions (4)

| Convention | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Consistency** | Once you choose a method, use it every year (don't change without a valid reason) | If AB Ltd changes its depreciation method every year to manipulate profit, its statements can't be compared |
| **Prudence / Conservatism** | Be **cautious** under uncertainty: **don't overstate** assets/income | Provide for a possible loss, but don't record an expected gain until it is certain |
| **Full Disclosure** | Disclose all **important (material)** information in the statements | Mention pending court cases and contingent liabilities in the notes |
| **Materiality** | Information is **material** if hiding or misstating it could change users' decisions | A ₹100 Cr company can expense a ₹500 stapler directly; no need to capitalize it |

## 6. Accounting Equation ⭐ (most important)

$$\textbf{Assets = Liabilities + Equity}$$

This is the foundation of **double-entry accounting**. It shows where the money for every asset came from:
- **Borrowed from outsiders** (Liabilities), or
- **Owner's money + accumulated profit** (Equity)

> 💡 **In simple words:** Equation **har transaction ke baad balanced rehni chahiye.** Ek side badla to doosri side bhi utna hi badlega (ya same side pe ek plus, ek minus).

### 6A. Assets: "What the business OWNS"
Resources **owned/controlled** by the business that will give **future economic benefits**.

| Current Assets (turn into cash within 12 months / operating cycle) | Non-Current Assets (benefit over a long period) |
|---|---|
| Cash, Bank balance | Buildings, Machinery |
| Accounts receivable (debtors) | Computers, Vehicles |
| Inventory | Long-term investments |
| Short-term investments | Intangible assets (patents, software) |
| Prepaid expenses | Right-of-use assets (things taken on lease) |

### 6B. Liabilities: "What the business OWES"
An **obligation** to an outside party.

| Current Liabilities (due within 12 months) | Non-Current Liabilities (due after 12 months) |
|---|---|
| Accounts payable (creditors) | Long-term bank loans |
| Salaries payable, Interest payable | Long-term bonds |
| Taxes payable | Certain lease liabilities |
| Short-term borrowings | |

### 6C. Equity: "The owner's residual claim"
$$\text{Equity = Assets − Liabilities}$$

Whatever is left after paying off all liabilities belongs to the owner (**residual claim**).

| Equity increases ⬆️ | Equity decreases ⬇️ |
|---|---|
| Owner's capital | Business losses |
| Retained profits | Drawings / Dividends |
| Additional owner contributions | |

- **Proprietorship:** `Equity = Owner's Capital + Profit − Drawings`
- **Company:** `Equity = Share Capital + Retained Earnings + Other Reserves`

### 6D. The 7 Elements and their link to Equity
Assets, Liabilities, Equity, Income, Expenses, Gains, Losses.

Income, expenses, gains and losses **affect equity**:
$$\text{Change in Equity (from operations) = Income − Expenses + Gains − Losses}$$

> ⚠️ The slide writes this as `Equity = Income − Expenses + Gains − Losses`. It actually gives the **change** in equity (i.e. profit), not total equity. Capital and drawings are added/subtracted separately.

> 💡 **In simple words:** **Income** = main business se kamai (cafe ki catering). **Gain** = side wali kamai, jaise purana asset **carrying amount se zyada** mein bechna. Same logic se **Expense vs Loss**.
> **Carrying amount** = the asset's current value in the books (cost − depreciation).

## 7. Solved Examples

### Example A: GreenBite Cafe (identify the elements)

| # | Transaction | Asset | Liability | Equity | Income | Expense | Gain | Loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ananya invests ₹8,00,000 in the bank account | ✅ | — | ✅ | — | — | — | — |
| 2 | ₹4,00,000 loan from the bank | ✅ | ✅ | — | — | — | — | — |
| 3 | ₹1,20,000 catering on credit + ₹50,000 expenses | ✅ | — | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | — | — |
| 4 | Fridge sold for ₹90,000 (carrying ₹70,000) | ✅ | — | ✅ | — | — | ✅ | — |
| 5 | ₹40,000 rent + coffee machine sold for ₹30,000 (carrying ₹45,000) | ✅ | — | ✅ | — | ✅ | — | ✅ |

**Why?**
1. Cash comes in (asset ⬆️); owner's capital (equity ⬆️).
2. Cash comes in (asset ⬆️); loan must be repaid (liability ⬆️). No effect on equity.
3. Receivable ₹1.2L (asset ⬆️) + income. ₹50k expenses paid in cash (asset ⬇️) + expense. Net profit ₹70k → equity ⬆️.
4. Cash +90k, fridge −70k → assets net +20k. **Gain ₹20,000** → equity ⬆️.
5. Rent = expense ₹40k. Machine: cash +30k, machine −45k → **Loss ₹15,000**. Both reduce equity ⬇️.

### Example C: Rahul's Cafe (Proprietorship, equation step-by-step)

| Step | Transaction | Assets (Cash) | = Liabilities | + Equity | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rahul starts the cafe with ₹5,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 0 | 5,00,000 | Capital |
| 2 | ₹3,00,000 cash expenses | 2,00,000 | 0 | 2,00,000 | −3L from both |
| 3 | ₹4,00,000 coffee sold for cash | 6,00,000 | 0 | 6,00,000 | +4L to both |
| 4 | Rahul withdraws ₹50,000 for personal use | 5,50,000 | 0 | 5,50,000 | **Drawings**, equity ⬇️ |

✔️ Check: `Equity = Capital 5L + Profit (4L − 3L) − Drawings 50k = ₹5,50,000` ✅

> ⚠️ The slide says "as cash expenses" in step 3. That's a typo; it is **cash sales**.

### Example D: TechStar Ltd (Company form)

| Step | Transaction | Cash | Receivables | = Liabilities | + Equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Issues shares, receives ₹10,00,000 | 10,00,000 | 0 | 0 | 10,00,000 (Share capital) |
| 2a | ₹8,00,000 credit sales | 10,00,000 | 8,00,000 | 0 | 18,00,000 |
| 2b | ₹5,00,000 credit expenses | 10,00,000 | 8,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 13,00,000 |
| 3 | ₹1,00,000 dividend paid | 9,00,000 | 8,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 12,00,000 |

✔️ Final check: **Assets ₹17,00,000 = Liabilities ₹5,00,000 + Equity ₹12,00,000** ✅
Equity = Share Capital 10L + Profit 3L − Dividend 1L = 12L.

> 💡 **In simple words:** Credit pe sale → cash nahi, **receivable** (asset) badhta hai. Credit pe kharcha → cash nahi, **payable** (liability) badhta hai. Dividend expense **nahi** hai — wo profit ka distribution hai, isliye seedha equity se ghatta hai.
> ⚠️ The slide labels step 2b as "₹8,00,000 credit expenses". The correct amount is ₹5,00,000.

## 8. Quick Revision 🧠

- **Accounting** = Record → Classify → Summarize → Interpret
- **Users:** Internal (management, employees) vs External (investors, banks, suppliers, govt)
- **Types:** Financial, Management, Cost, Tax, Audit, Forensic
- **Concepts:** Entity, Going Concern, Money Measurement, Period, Historical Cost, Dual Aspect, Accrual, Matching, Realisation
- **Conventions:** Consistency, Prudence, Full Disclosure, Materiality
- **Golden rule:** `Assets = Liabilities + Equity` (always balanced)
- **Equity ⬆️** capital, profit, gains · **Equity ⬇️** losses, expenses, drawings/dividends

## 9. MCQs (Mid–Advanced Level) 📝

**Q1.** XYZ Ltd buys machinery worth ₹5,00,000 **on credit**. What is the effect on the accounting equation?
- A) Assets ⬆️, Liabilities ⬆️
- B) Assets unchanged, Liabilities ⬆️
- C) Assets ⬆️, Expenses ⬆️
- D) Assets ⬆️, Equity ⬆️

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Machinery (asset) +5L, and the amount owed to the supplier (liability) +5L. Machinery is a non-current asset, not an expense. 📖 Section 6

**Q2.** March salaries of ₹60,000 are paid to employees on 5 April. The FY ends on 31 March. What appears in March's books?
- A) Nothing, because payment was made in April
- B) Expense of ₹60,000 in March, and Salaries Payable of ₹60,000 as a liability
- C) Expense in April, liability in March
- D) Prepaid salary (asset) of ₹60,000

> ✅ **Answer: B.** **Accrual concept:** the service was received in March, so the expense belongs to March. Since it is unpaid on 31 March, it is a current liability. 📖 Section 5A-7

**Q3.** A gym receives ₹24,000 on 1 January as advance for a 12-month membership. How much revenue is recognised by 31 March, and how is the rest treated?
- A) ₹0 revenue, because the service is not complete
- B) ₹6,000 revenue; ₹18,000 liability (unearned revenue)
- C) ₹24,000 revenue
- D) ₹6,000 revenue; ₹18,000 equity

> ✅ **Answer: B.** **Revenue recognition:** ₹2,000 is earned each month, so 3 months = ₹6,000. The remaining ₹18,000 of service is still owed, making it an **obligation** (liability) to the member. Same as the Netflix example. 📖 Section 5A-9

**Q4.** A trader buys 500 units @ ₹200 and sells 300 units @ ₹350. Under the matching concept, what are Gross Profit and Closing Inventory?
- A) GP ₹5,000; Inventory ₹0
- B) GP ₹45,000; Inventory ₹40,000
- C) GP ₹1,05,000; Inventory ₹40,000
- D) GP ₹45,000; Inventory ₹60,000

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Sales = 300 × 350 = ₹1,05,000. COGS = 300 × 200 = ₹60,000. GP = ₹45,000. Remaining 200 units × 200 = ₹40,000 inventory. Option A wrongly treats the entire purchase (₹1,00,000) as an expense. 📖 FashionHub example

**Q5.** A machine with a carrying amount of ₹2,00,000 is sold for ₹1,60,000 cash. Which statement is correct?
- A) Loss ₹40,000; total assets down ₹40,000, equity down ₹40,000
- B) Income ₹1,60,000; total assets unchanged
- C) Loss ₹40,000; liabilities ⬆️
- D) Gain ₹1,60,000; equity ⬆️

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Cash +1.6L, machine −2L, so assets fall by 40k net. It is a loss because the asset was sold below its carrying amount, and a loss reduces equity. It isn't income, since selling machines is not the main business. 📖 Section 6D

**Q6.** A company bought land for ₹50 lakh in 2015. Its market value today is ₹1.2 crore. Under the traditional **historical cost concept**, at what value was the land initially recorded?
- A) ₹50 lakh
- B) ₹1.2 crore
- C) ₹70 lakh (unrealised gain)
- D) ₹85 lakh (average)

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Assets are recorded at **acquisition cost**. The rise in market value (₹70L) is an unrealised gain, which prudence also says not to record. 📖 Section 5A-5

**Q7.** A company expects that a customer's ₹2L bill may not be paid. It also expects to receive ₹5L from a pending lawsuit. What should it do under **prudence**?
- A) Record both
- B) Record only the expected ₹5L gain
- C) Ignore both
- D) Provide for the possible ₹2L loss; don't record the ₹5L gain until it is certain

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Prudence says anticipate losses, not gains. This prevents assets/income from being overstated. 📖 Section 5B

**Q8.** Rohan's proprietorship has opening capital of ₹6,00,000. During the year: additional capital ₹50,000, profit ₹2,50,000, drawings ₹1,00,000. What is closing equity?
- A) ₹8,00,000
- B) ₹7,50,000
- C) ₹8,50,000
- D) ₹9,00,000

> ✅ **Answer: A.** 6,00,000 + 50,000 + 2,50,000 − 1,00,000 = **₹8,00,000**. 📖 Section 6C

**Q9.** A company has total assets of ₹25L, current liabilities of ₹4L and non-current liabilities of ₹9L. What is equity?
- A) ₹21L
- B) ₹12L
- C) ₹16L
- D) ₹38L

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Equity = Assets − **Total** Liabilities = 25 − (4 + 9) = ₹12L. The common mistake is subtracting only one type of liability. 📖 Section 6C

**Q10.** Which of the following transactions leaves **total assets unchanged**?
- A) Pays a creditor ₹30,000
- B) Takes a ₹2L bank loan
- C) Owner invests ₹1L
- D) Buys ₹50,000 of inventory for cash

> ✅ **Answer: D.** One asset (cash) ⬇️ and another (inventory) ⬆️ — an asset swap. In B and C assets rise; in A assets fall along with liabilities. 📖 Section 6

**Q11.** The owner takes goods worth ₹20,000 from the shop for home use. Which concept applies and what is the effect?
- A) Money measurement; expense ⬆️
- B) Matching; COGS ⬆️
- C) Business entity; drawings → assets ⬇️ and equity ⬇️
- D) Going concern; no effect

> ✅ **Answer: C.** The business and owner are separate, so the owner's personal use is **drawings**, not a business expense. Inventory ⬇️ and equity ⬇️. 📖 Section 5A-1

**Q12.** A company changes its depreciation method every year to smooth profits. Which convention is violated?
- A) Consistency
- B) Going concern
- C) Materiality
- D) Full disclosure

> ✅ **Answer: A.** A method should change only for a valid reason. Changing it every year makes statements non-comparable. Same as the AB Ltd example. 📖 Section 5B

**Q13.** A company with ₹100 crore revenue expenses a ₹500 stapler directly instead of recording it as an asset. Which convention justifies this?
- A) Historical cost
- B) Prudence
- C) Consistency
- D) Materiality

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Such a small amount won't change any user's decision, so it is immaterial. 📖 Section 5B

**Q14.** Which of the following is **NOT** a current asset?
- A) Prepaid insurance
- B) Short-term investments
- C) Accounts receivable
- D) Right-of-use asset

> ✅ **Answer: D.** A right-of-use asset (the right to use leased property) gives benefits over a long period, so it is **non-current**. Prepaid expenses are current assets. 📖 Section 6A

**Q15.** A company is expected to be **liquidated** next month. Which concept no longer applies?
- A) Going concern
- B) Business entity
- C) Accounting period
- D) Dual aspect

> ✅ **Answer: A.** Going concern assumes the business will continue. Liquidation breaks that assumption, and assets must be viewed at realisable (sale) value. 📖 Section 5A-2

**Q16.** A company issues shares for ₹20L cash, makes ₹6L of credit sales, incurs ₹4L of credit expenses and pays a ₹2L cash dividend. What are total assets?
- A) ₹26L
- B) ₹24L
- C) ₹28L
- D) ₹20L

> ✅ **Answer: B.** Cash = 20 − 2 = 18L, Receivables = 6L, so Assets = **24L**. Check: Liabilities 4L + Equity (20 + 6 − 4 − 2 = 20L) = 24L ✅. Same pattern as TechStar. 📖 Example D

**Q17.** Which statement about dividend payment is **correct**?
- A) A dividend increases liabilities
- B) A dividend is a distribution of profit that reduces both cash and equity
- C) A dividend has no effect on equity
- D) A dividend is an expense that goes to the P&L

> ✅ **Answer: B.** A dividend is not an expense. It returns profit to the owners, so it reduces equity directly (like drawings in a proprietorship). 📖 Example D

**Q18.** Goods were delivered on 28 March and payment arrived on 10 April. Another customer paid an advance on 25 March for goods to be delivered in April. The FY ends on 31 March. What is March's revenue?
- A) Both transactions
- B) Only the 25 March advance
- C) Only the 28 March delivery
- D) Neither

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Revenue is recognised when the **performance obligation is satisfied**, i.e. on delivery. The advance is still a liability because the goods haven't been delivered. 📖 Section 5A-9

**Q19.** A bank checks a company's **liquidity and solvency** before approving a loan. What type of user is the bank, and which output will it review?
- A) Internal user; management accounting reports
- B) Internal user; cost accounting
- C) External user; (audited) financial accounting statements
- D) External user; tax accounting only

> ✅ **Answer: C.** A bank is an external user and reviews audited financial statements (Balance Sheet, P&L, Cash Flow). Management accounting reports are internal. 📖 Sections 3 & 4

**Q20.** Why can "book profit" and "taxable profit" differ?
- A) Because they are in different currencies
- B) Because auditing is different
- C) Because financial accounting follows accounting standards while tax accounting follows tax laws

**Q21.** A cafe provides catering worth ₹1,00,000 on credit and buys and uses ₹40,000 of ingredients for cash. What is the net effect?
- A) Assets +₹1,00,000; Equity +₹1,00,000
- B) Assets +₹60,000; Liabilities +₹60,000
- C) Assets +₹60,000; Equity +₹60,000
- D) Assets −₹40,000; Equity −₹40,000

> ✅ **Answer: C.** Receivable +1L, Cash −40k, so Assets net +60k. Income 1L − Expense 40k = profit 60k, so Equity +60k. The equation balances. 📖 GreenBite #3

**Q22.** Which of the following is a **Gain**, not Income?
- A) A bakery sells cakes
- B) A cafe provides catering
- C) A consultant earns fees
- D) A bakery sells an old van for ₹50,000 above its carrying amount

> ✅ **Answer: D.** Earnings from the main business (cakes, catering, consulting) are **income**. A side activity, such as selling an old asset above its carrying amount, gives a **gain**. 📖 Section 6D
